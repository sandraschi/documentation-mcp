## 1. The Moreno-Gama Case: Facts and Analysis

On April 9, 2026, Daniel Moreno-Gama, a 38-year-old PauseAI volunteer and self-described "Butlerian," was arrested in Palo Alto, California, following two incidents: a Molotov cocktail thrown at Sam Altman's front gate, and a chair thrown against the entrance of OpenAI's office building. Both acts were more performative than destructive — no casualties, limited property damage. But the trajectory matters. A three-part manifesto titled "Your Last Warning" was recovered at the time of arrest. The Federal Criminal Complaint (United States v. Moreno-Gama, N.D. Cal., April 2026) charges multiple counts of arson, conspiracy, and domestic terrorism.

### 1.1 What the Manifesto Argues

The manifesto's core argument, reconstructed from publicly available excerpts:

1. Artificial intelligence development constitutes an existential threat to humanity.
2. Democratic and regulatory processes have failed to constrain AI development.
3. Therefore, direct action against AI infrastructure — including property destruction — is morally justified.
4. The "Butlerian Jihad" (from Frank Herbert's *Dune*) provides a historical template for resistance against thinking machines.

### 1.2 The Radicalization Timeline

The radicalization pathway from mainstream AI safety concern to operational violence can be reconstructed from court documents, online activity records, and PauseAI's own statements:

- **2022–2023**: Moreno-Gama participates in LessWrong forums and AI safety discussions. Engages seriously with Yudkowsky's arguments about existential risk.
- **2023–2024**: Joins PauseAI as a volunteer. Becomes increasingly active in Discord discussions. Begins sharing content from PauseAI and similar groups on social media.
- **Late 2024**: Expelled from PauseAI Discord for "escalating rhetoric." PauseAI's April 2026 statement confirms this and notes he was "never an official member" after this point.
- **2025**: Begins posting on Instagram under the handle "butlerian_jihadist." Content shifts from civil disobedience advocacy to direct action justification.
- **Early 2026**: Alleged planning and preparation for the April attacks.
- **April 9, 2026**: Arrest.

### 1.3 Analytical Observations

The progression from LessWrong participant → PauseAI volunteer → expelled radical → operational actor follows a recognizable radicalization pattern familiar to counterterrorism analysis. The key transition point is not the initial belief in AI risk (which is shared by many non-violent researchers) but the rejection of institutional and democratic constraints — the conviction that existing systems have "failed" and that extraordinary measures are therefore justified.

This pattern has historical precedents discussed in Section 6. The critical analytical distinction is between *belief* (which is protected speech) and *operationalization* (which is not). The question for this debate is whether the intellectual infrastructure of anti-AI discourse — the p(doom) framing, the extinction rhetoric, the Dune-as-manual interpretation — creates conditions conducive to this transition, and if so, what responsibility that infrastructure's architects bear.

## 2. Environmental Claims: Water, Energy, and Emissions

Environmental concerns about AI infrastructure are legitimate and deserve engagement on their merits. Several specific claims, however, require factual correction.

### 2.1 The Water Consumption Correction

**The claim**: "A Google data center near Santiago uses more than 1,000× the water of the entire local population." (Hao, *Empire of AI*, 2025, p. 288)

**The correction**: In December 2025, Karen Hao issued a correction statement on her website identifying a unit conversion error in the book: liters per second were misread as cubic meters per hour. The corrected figure is approximately equal to the annual residential water use of a city of ~88,000 people — not a factor of 1,000 times the local population. The original claim was off by three orders of magnitude.

**Independent verification**: Andy Masley's Substack analysis (November 2025) independently identified the error prior to Hao's correction, providing detailed methodology for the calculation.

**What remains true**: Data centers do consume water for cooling. The environmental footprint of AI infrastructure is non-trivial and merits ongoing monitoring. The corrected figure does not eliminate the concern — it brings it into the range where reasoned policy discussion is possible rather than where apocalyptic claims bypass discussion entirely.

### 2.2 The Drinkable Water Claim

**The claim**: "AI data centers will consume 1.7 trillion gallons of drinkable water by 2027" (various media, citing Goldman Sachs / Morgan Stanley projections).

**The correction**: The original study specifies that approximately 3% of projected water use would be potable/drinkable water, with roughly 90% returned to the source unaffected. The "drinkable water" framing requires compounding misreadings: treating total water throughput as consumption, treating all water as potable, and ignoring return rates.

**Source**: Andy Masley, "The AI Water Issue Is Fake," Substack (2025); original Goldman Sachs report (2023).

### 2.3 Energy Consumption in Context

Global data center electricity consumption is approximately 1–1.5% of total global electricity use (IEA, 2024). AI workloads represent a growing fraction of this total but remain a subset. By comparison:

- Residential air conditioning: ~10% of global electricity
- Aluminum smelting: ~3%
- Cryptocurrency mining: ~0.5–1%

The trajectory matters — AI energy demand is growing — but the *current* figures do not support claims of imminent grid collapse or catastrophic resource competition. This is an area where projected future consumption is frequently presented as current reality.

## 3. Extinction Risk: p(doom) and Falsifiability

The claim that artificial intelligence will cause human extinction with high probability — colloquially "p(doom)" — is the central intellectual claim of the radical anti-AI position. It warrants careful examination not because it is plausible but because it is the belief that justifies every subsequent escalation.

### 3.1 What the Claim States

Eliezer Yudkowsky and associated writers on LessWrong have consistently argued that the probability of AI-caused human extinction exceeds 90%, with some estimates reaching 99%. Yudkowsky's 2023 *Time* article, "Pausing AI Developments Isn't Enough. We Need to Shut It All Down," explicitly proposes military action against data centers as a proportionate response to this level of risk.

### 3.2 The Falsifiability Problem

A probability estimate requires either:
- A reference class of similar events from which to derive a frequency, or
- A causal model whose predictions can be tested against observation

p(doom) has neither. There is no reference class of artificial general intelligences causing human extinction — it would be a unique event. There is no falsifiable causal model — any outcome (AI kills everyone, AI doesn't kill everyone yet, AI appears safe but is secretly plotting) can be made consistent with the prior. The estimate is unfalsifiable by construction.

This does not make the concern *wrong*. It makes it *non-scientific* — a statement of conviction rather than an empirical claim. A person can hold any p(doom) they wish, but they cannot claim the authority of science or probability theory for that number.

### 3.3 Expert Disagreement

Credentialed AI safety researchers explicitly distance themselves from Yudkowsky's probability estimates:

- **Stuart Russell** (UC Berkeley, co-author of the standard AI textbook): Argues for specific, bounded alignment concerns in *Human Compatible* (2019) without claiming extinction probability.
- **Geoffrey Hinton** (Turing Award, "Godfather of AI"): Has expressed serious concern about AI risk and left Google to speak freely, but has not endorsed the 90%+ extinction probability framing.
- **Yoshua Bengio** (Turing Award): Advocates for regulation and safety research; participates in the International Scientific Report on Advanced AI Safety; does not endorse p(doom) framing.

The existence of serious, credentialed concern about AI safety is not in dispute. What is in dispute is whether this concern supports the specific claim that extinction is near-certain, and whether that claim, even if believed, justifies the actions it is being used to defend.

### 3.4 Yudkowsky's Track Record

Yudkowsky has made public predictions for over two decades. Notable among them:

- Predicted in 2008 that AGI would be achieved within "5–10 years" (deadline: 2013–2018). This did not occur.
- Predicted that recursive self-improvement would produce an "intelligence explosion" once a threshold was crossed. No such explosion has occurred through multiple generations of increasingly capable models.
- Consistently argued that capabilities we have since achieved (GPT-4 class language models, multimodal systems) would not be possible without solving alignment first. They have been achieved without solving alignment, and without causing extinction.

None of this proves future predictions wrong. It establishes that the predictor's track record does not warrant high confidence.

## 4. The "Dune as Manual" Misreading

### 4.1 The Claim

The Butlerian Jihad — the fictional war against thinking machines in Frank Herbert's *Dune* universe — is presented in anti-AI communities as a template for real-world resistance. Moreno-Gama's Instagram handle ("butlerian_jihadist") and manifesto explicitly reference this interpretation. The "Butlerism" movement positions itself as the legitimate heir to Herbert's vision.

### 4.2 Herbert's Actual Position

Frank Herbert was unambiguous about his intentions. In collected interviews (*The Maker of Dune*, 1987), he states:

- The Butlerian Jihad is presented in the series as a *catastrophe* — a religious war that humanity barely survived and whose consequences reverberate for millennia.
- The post-Jihad prohibition on thinking machines functions only because Herbert invented biological and pharmacological substitutes that do not exist in reality: Mentats (humans trained from childhood as organic computers), the Bene Gesserit (genetic memory and superhuman bodily control), Guild Navigators (prescience through spice saturation), and melange itself (a substance with no real-world analogue).
- The entire series is a warning against messianic movements and charismatic leaders, not an endorsement of machine-smashing.

### 4.3 The Literary Evidence

The *Dune* series contains multiple internal critiques of the Butlerian prohibition:

- The prohibition on thinking machines does not prevent the emergence of tyrannical systems — the Padishah Empire, the God-Emperor's reign, and the Honored Matres are all human-created tyrannies that exist without AI.
- The Bene Gesserit's breeding program and the Spacing Guild's monopoly on navigation are themselves forms of dehumanizing control that the machine prohibition enables.
- Herbert's later books increasingly focus on the problem of human-created systems of control, suggesting that the machine prohibition was a red herring — the danger was always human institutions.

The reading of *Dune* as an anti-AI manual requires ignoring the author's stated intent, the internal logic of the fictional universe, and the thematic arc of the series. It is not an interpretation — it is appropriation.

## 5. The Radicalization Pipeline: Intellectual Infrastructure to Violence

### 5.1 The Recognizable Pattern

Counterterrorism analysis identifies a common structure in radicalization pathways:

1. **Grievance formation**: A legitimate concern or fear is identified and amplified.
2. **Institutional delegitimization**: Existing processes (democratic, regulatory, scientific) are declared corrupt, captured, or hopelessly inadequate.
3. **Exceptionalism**: The threat is framed as so unique and so severe that normal ethical constraints do not apply.
4. **Operationalization**: Direct action — initially non-violent, potentially escalating — becomes the logical conclusion of the previous steps.

This pattern describes multiple historical movements: anarchist terrorism (late 19th century), anti-abortion violence (1980s–1990s), jihadist radicalization (1990s–2010s), eco-terrorism (1990s–2000s), and now anti-AI radicalization (2020s). The content of the grievance changes; the structure is consistent.

### 5.2 Applying the Framework to Anti-AI Radicalization

**Grievance formation**: AI risk is real — respected researchers have genuine concerns about alignment, safety, and governance. This legitimate concern base creates a pool of potential recruits who are not initially radicalized.

**Institutional delegitimization**: Yudkowsky and associated writers have consistently argued that democratic processes, corporate governance, and academic AI research are structurally incapable of addressing AI risk. The claim is not that these institutions are *currently failing* — it is that they are *inherently incapable*. This is the critical transition point.

**Exceptionalism**: The p(doom) framing serves this function precisely. If AI has a 99% chance of killing everyone, and everyone who disagrees is either deluded or complicit, then normal constraints on behavior are not just optional — they are suicidal. A 99% extinction probability justifies actions that a 1% or even 10% probability would not. This is why the specific magnitude of the claim matters.

**Operationalization**: Yudkowsky's 2023 *Time* article openly proposed bombing data centers. The Dune-as-manual reading provides a fictional template. The movement has not (yet) produced large-scale violence beyond Moreno-Gama, but the intellectual infrastructure for escalation is in place.

### 5.3 The Responsibility Question

The architects of anti-AI rhetoric did not tell Moreno-Gama to commit arson. But they did tell him — and everyone else reading — that AI will almost certainly kill everyone, that democratic institutions cannot stop it, and that bombing data centers is a proportionate response. The distance between that intellectual framework and an individual deciding to act on it is shorter than its architects seem willing to acknowledge.

This is not a legal argument about incitement. It is an analytical observation about radicalization dynamics. The PauseAI leadership recognized this when they expelled Moreno-Gama for "escalating rhetoric" — they understood, correctly, that the line between advocacy and incitement is thin and that their own rhetoric was creating conditions they could not control.

## 6. Historical Parallels: Technological Panics

Opposition to transformative technologies is not new. Examining past technological panics provides context for evaluating current claims about AI.

### 6.1 The Printing Press (15th Century)

The Catholic Church and secular authorities both attempted to restrict the printing press. The concern was genuine: mass literacy and the circulation of ideas would destabilize existing power structures. They were correct — it did. It also enabled the scientific revolution, democratic governance, and universal education. The printing press produced both Luther's 95 Theses and the Thirty Years' War. The correct historical judgment is not that the printing press was unambiguously good, but that attempts to suppress it were futile and counterproductive.

### 6.2 Industrial Automation (19th Century)

The Luddite movement (1811–1816) destroyed textile machinery in the belief that automation would eliminate skilled labor and immiserate the working class. The concern was genuine: mechanization did displace specific categories of skilled workers, causing real suffering. But the long-term effect was not the elimination of work — it was the transformation of work, the creation of new categories of labor, and a dramatic increase in material living standards. The Luddites were right about the short-term pain and wrong about the long-term trajectory.

### 6.3 Nuclear Weapons (1945–Present)

The atomic bomb is the closest historical parallel to p(doom) reasoning. In 1945, credible scientists argued that nuclear weapons represented an existential threat to humanity. They were right in the sense that nuclear war *could* end civilization. They were wrong in predicting that it *would*. The nuclear non-proliferation regime, arms control treaties, and deterrence theory prevented the predicted extinction. The lesson is not that existential threats should be dismissed; it is that institutional responses can be effective even against threats that initially appear insurmountable.

### 6.4 Genetic Engineering (1970s–2000s)

The Asilomar Conference (1975) and subsequent regulatory frameworks for recombinant DNA research are frequently cited as a model for AI governance. The concern was genuine: genetic modification could create dangerous organisms. Scientists voluntarily imposed a moratorium, developed safety protocols, and established regulatory frameworks. The technology proceeded, with safeguards, and has produced medical treatments, agricultural improvements, and fundamental scientific knowledge. This is the precedent that suggests institutional management of powerful technologies is possible.

### 6.5 The Internet and Social Media (1990s–Present)

Early internet optimism predicted global democratization, universal access to knowledge, and the end of information scarcity. Much of this happened. It also produced mass surveillance, algorithmic radicalization, election interference, and the erosion of shared factual reality. This is the most cautionary of the historical parallels: the optimistic predictions and the pessimistic predictions were both partially correct, and the actual outcome was shaped by specific institutional and regulatory choices — not by the technology's inherent properties.

### 6.6 Pattern Analysis

Across these cases, several patterns emerge:

1. **Both optimists and pessimists are usually wrong about magnitude.** Technologies produce effects that are smaller than the most extreme predictions in both directions.
2. **Institutional responses matter more than technological properties.** Printing presses, nuclear weapons, and recombinant DNA all had their outcomes shaped by the regulatory and institutional framework built around them.
3. **The most confident predictors have the worst track records.** Those who claimed with certainty that the internet would democratize everything, or that nuclear weapons would end everything, were both wrong.
4. **The precautionary principle is not self-evidently correct.** Had it been applied to the printing press, nuclear physics, or genetic engineering, it would have prevented both the worst outcomes and the best ones. The question is always: *compared to what?*

## 7. The Positive Case: What AI Development Enables

The debate over AI risk frequently assumes that AI development's benefits are either negligible or achievable through alternative means. This section documents current and near-term applications that would not exist without AI research.

### 7.1 Medical Research

- **AlphaFold 3** (DeepMind, 2024): Predicted structures for over 200 million proteins, including all known proteins in the human body. This capability accelerates drug discovery for neglected diseases, antibiotic resistance, and cancer. The 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry was awarded for related work.
- **Medical imaging**: AI systems now match or exceed radiologist performance on specific diagnostic tasks (breast cancer screening, retinal disease detection, lung nodule identification). These systems are being deployed in settings with limited access to specialists.
- **Drug repurposing**: AI screening of existing drugs for new applications has identified candidates for ALS, Alzheimer's, and rare genetic disorders. These discoveries would be economically infeasible through traditional trial-and-error methods.

### 7.2 Climate and Environmental Science

- **Climate modeling**: AI-enhanced climate models reduce simulation time from months to hours, enabling more scenarios, higher resolution, and better uncertainty quantification.
- **Materials science**: AI prediction of material properties accelerates the development of better batteries, solar cells, and carbon capture materials.
- **Agricultural optimization**: AI-driven precision agriculture reduces water use, fertilizer runoff, and pesticide application while maintaining or increasing yields.

### 7.3 Accessibility

- **Real-time captioning and translation**: Enables deaf and hard-of-hearing individuals to participate in spoken conversation and access audio content.
- **Screen readers and image description**: AI-generated descriptions of images and visual environments for blind and low-vision users.
- **Communication aids**: Predictive text and speech generation for individuals with ALS, cerebral palsy, aphasia, and other conditions affecting communication.

### 7.4 Scientific Discovery

- **Mathematics**: AI systems have contributed to new proofs in knot theory and representation theory, collaborating with human mathematicians rather than replacing them.
- **Physics**: AI analysis of particle physics data at CERN enables detection of rare events that would be missed by conventional analysis.
- **Biology**: Beyond protein folding, AI systems are analyzing genomic data at scales that would require centuries of human effort.

### 7.5 The Counter-Question

For each of these applications, the question posed by the radical anti-AI position is: *Would you trade this benefit for a 99% reduction in extinction risk?*

The question reveals the structure of the disagreement. If you believe the extinction risk is 99%, almost any benefit is insufficient. If you believe the risk is closer to the estimates produced by the International Scientific Report on Advanced AI Safety (2025) — serious, manageable, requiring institutional response — the calculus changes entirely.

The debate is not really about water consumption or job displacement or Dune interpretations. It is about whether the extinction framing is accurate enough to justify the actions it is being used to defend. That is a question about which reasonable people can disagree. It is not a question that should be settled by the person with the strongest conviction or the most apocalyptic rhetoric.
