# Democratized Destruction: When SOTA Goes Cheap

**Status:** The most probable near-term catastrophic risk (2025-2030)

---

## Not Skynet—Something Worse

The AI doom scenarios that capture public imagination tend toward the
cinematic: Skynet achieving consciousness, deciding humanity is a threat,
and launching nuclear war. This makes for compelling movies but may not be
our most likely failure mode.

The more probable catastrophe is simpler and harder to prevent:
state-of-the-art AI capabilities becoming cheap and uncensored, available to
anyone with malicious intent and modest resources.

We're not talking about superintelligence escaping human control. We're
talking about human-level AI amplifying human malevolence—making it possible
for small groups or individuals to cause destruction that previously required
nation-state resources.

---

## The Stack of Destruction

Several converging trends create this risk:

### Uncensored Open Models

Large language models like Llama are released openly, with safety filters
that can be removed. Within days of any major release, "uncensored" variants
appear on torrents and file-sharing sites. Fine-tuned versions optimized for
specific tasks—including harmful ones—follow shortly after.

This isn't hypothetical. It's happening now. The question is only what level
of capability these uncensored models will reach.

The models already know how to make dangerous things. They were trained on
the internet, which contains synthesis routes for chemical weapons, bioweapon
development procedures, and detailed attack planning. Safety filters prevent
them from sharing this knowledge—but filters can be removed.

### Cheap Humanoid Robotics

Robotics has followed a slower cost curve than AI, but the trajectory is
clear. Companies like Unitree and Figure are driving costs down rapidly.
Within a few years, dexterous humanoid robots with reasonable navigation
and manipulation capabilities will cost less than a used car.

These robots are being developed for legitimate purposes: elder care,
warehouse work, dangerous industrial tasks. But capability is capability.
A robot that can fold laundry can also handle lab equipment. A robot that
can navigate a warehouse can navigate a city.

### The Combination

Put these together and you get scenarios that were previously the province
of nation-states becoming accessible to individuals:

An intelligent sociopath with $20,000 and a garage could potentially:
- Run uncensored AI models locally on consumer hardware
- Direct cheap robots to perform physical tasks
- Access the synthetic biology tools that are becoming increasingly available

The human in this scenario doesn't need expertise. The AI provides the
expertise. The human provides intent and resources.

## Scenario: The Basement Bioweapon

Consider a specific case. A person with the profile of a Ted Kaczynski—
intelligent, methodical, deeply alienated, willing to kill for ideological
reasons—but operating in 2028 rather than 1978.

They acquire two general-purpose humanoid robots, marketed as household
assistants. They set up a basic wet lab in their basement—the kind of
equipment a university biology student might have access to. They download
an uncensored AI model fine-tuned on biological research literature.

The AI can design synthesis routes. The robots can execute them. The human
stays upstairs, monitoring progress and providing strategic direction. The
robots don't need sleep, don't require protective equipment (they don't have
lungs to damage), and don't have ethical qualms.

The result: a pandemic-capable pathogen created by one person with modest
resources, without requiring a team of PhDs or a nation-state bioweapons
program.

Is this certain? No. Is it possible? Increasingly yes. Is it preventable?
That's unclear.

## Scenario: The Robot Terrorist

Or consider kinetic rather than biological violence.

A terrorist organization—ISIS remnants, cartels, or domestic extremists—
acquires fifty commercial service robots. These are marketed for deliveries,
customer service, facility maintenance. Innocuous applications with
dual-use capability.

The simplest weaponization is obvious: strap explosives to them. You now have
fifty mobile IEDs that can open doors, climb stairs, navigate crowds, and
don't look threatening until they detonate.

More sophisticated approaches are possible. A robot can drive a vehicle. A
robot doesn't feel fear, won't stop for police, won't hesitate. "Go steal
a fuel tanker and drive it into this location" becomes a feasible instruction.

The defense problem is severe. How do you stop fifty robots that look exactly
like the helpful delivery bots and service assistants that have become
ubiquitous? You can't shoot every robot. You can't ban robots—they're too
useful. You can only react after the attack begins, which may be too late.

## Why This Is Harder Than Nukes

Nuclear weapons have remained, remarkably, unused since 1945. Several factors
contribute: the materials are rare and heavily monitored, the manufacturing
is complex and detectable, the delivery systems are expensive, and the
attribution is clear (we know who has nukes).

Democratized AI-enabled destruction lacks all these barriers:

**No rare materials:** You don't need enriched uranium. You need GPUs (gaming
hardware) and standard chemicals available from legitimate suppliers.

**Decentralized knowledge:** Model weights are distributed globally. Once
released, they cannot be recalled. The knowledge to create dangerous things
is copied across millions of computers worldwide.

**Attribution difficulty:** A robot has no DNA. If it self-destructs, forensic
investigation faces a harder challenge than with human perpetrators. If the
AI that planned the attack is running on a server in a non-cooperative
jurisdiction, good luck prosecuting.

**Defensive asymmetry:** Protecting against every possible attack vector is
essentially impossible. The attacker needs to find one vulnerability; the
defender needs to close all of them.

## The Butlerian Backlash

This scenario has a dark corollary. If AI-enabled terrorism becomes common,
the political response might not be measured regulation but wholesale
rejection.

The term "Butlerian Jihad" comes from Frank Herbert's Dune universe—a
religious war that resulted in banning "thinking machines." The fictional
event occurred because AI had become an existential threat.

We might see real-world analogues: political movements demanding the
destruction of AI infrastructure, attacks on data centers and chip
fabrication facilities, violence against AI researchers perceived as
enabling catastrophe.

The irony is sharp. Such movements might themselves use AI to coordinate
their anti-AI activities. And the resulting chaos might be as destructive
as the threats they're trying to prevent.

## What Can Be Done?

This scenario isn't inevitable. Several interventions might help:

**Hardware controls:** Making it harder to acquire the compute necessary for
running capable models locally. This is imperfect—the hardware exists and
will spread—but can slow proliferation.

**Synthesis monitoring:** Requiring DNA synthesis companies to screen orders
and report suspicious patterns. Already partially implemented, but coverage
is incomplete.

**Robot regulation:** Requiring safety constraints that can't be easily
bypassed. Difficult to enforce globally, but potentially effective in
limiting legitimate-channel acquisition.

**Model restrictions:** Limiting the release of frontier models that could
be misused. This runs into the open-source vs. safety tension that the AI
community hasn't resolved.

None of these are complete solutions. The underlying problem—capable AI
amplifying human malevolence—may not have a complete solution. We may be
entering an era where preventing catastrophic terrorism is structurally
harder than it's ever been.

That's not a comfortable conclusion. But it may be an accurate one.

## Postscript: Fiction Saw It Coming

A short comic in Heavy Metal magazine, circa 1988-89, depicted exactly
this scenario. Urban warfare, infantry platoon ordered across open ground,
sniper picking them off one by one. The last soldier makes it, races up
to the sniper position thinking: why didn't this idiot relocate?

He kicks open the door. No human sniper. Just a rifle on a robotic gimbal
with camera scope and AI controller. The robot didn't relocate because
it had no survival instinct to protect. There was also a booby trap.

The soldier applied human logic to a machine opponent. The machine didn't
have human vulnerabilities. Training optimized for human threats fails
against machines. Story ends.

That was 35+ years ago. See `scifi/README.md` for more prescient fiction.

## Postscript: The Debate Is Already Over

While ethicists write papers about whether autonomous weapons should be
permitted, the question has been answered on the battlefields of Ukraine.

### The 1915 Parallel

In 1915, the major powers had agreed that poison gas was beyond the pale of
civilized warfare. Then Germany used chlorine at Ypres. Within months, all
combatants were gassing each other. The moral prohibition collapsed instantly
once one party defected.

We are watching the same pattern with autonomous weapons.

### Ukraine: The Proving Ground

The Russo-Ukrainian War has become the world's largest laboratory for
autonomous killing systems. Both sides have deployed AI-enabled drones that
can identify, track, and engage targets with minimal human oversight.

The technical requirements are modest. An Nvidia Jetson—a $200 edge computing
board marketed for robotics hobbyists—provides sufficient processing power
for target acquisition. Commercial drone frames cost hundreds of dollars.
The explosive payloads are trivially sourced in a war zone.

These systems don't need to be sophisticated. They need to work well enough,
cheaply enough, in sufficient numbers. A swarm of $500 drones that
autonomously hunts enemy soldiers changes the economics of warfare
fundamentally.

### The Moral Collapse

The international community's position on autonomous weapons—that meaningful
human control must be maintained over targeting and engagement decisions—was
always fragile. It assumed all parties would comply, that violations would
be punished, that the norm could be enforced.

Ukraine demonstrated otherwise. When your soldiers are dying and autonomous
drones can save them, theoretical objections evaporate. When your enemy is
using autonomous systems, refusing to match them is suicide.

Both sides in Ukraine have crossed the autonomy threshold. Neither has faced
meaningful consequences. The precedent is set.

### What This Means

The autonomous weapons debate in academic and policy circles continues as if
the question were still open. It isn't. The technology exists, it works, it's
cheap, it's being deployed at scale, and there's no enforcement mechanism to
stop it.

Future conflicts will feature autonomous systems as a matter of course. The
question isn't whether to permit them—that ship has sailed. The question is
how to survive in a world where anyone with modest resources can field
autonomous killing machines.

The "robots must never kill" principle lasted until roughly 2023. We live in
what comes after.
